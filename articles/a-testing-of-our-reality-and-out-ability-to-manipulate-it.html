Why China Can BBQ in Space (But NASA Can’t)

What is the hardest to shoot down, cruise missiles or ICBMs?

So… I would say it’s ICBMs, but partly not for the reasons you’ve probably got in mind.

ICBMs are not tactical weapons- they are basically 100% nuclear tipped, and even if they weren’t, anyone who sees them coming is going to have to assume they are. Further, almost all of the modern ones are MIRV’d. They are also exo-atmospheric targets before MIRV (and likely, decoy) separation, which alone makes them very hard to get at. During reentry they come in at incredible speed.

But the real problem with shooting down ICBMs is the scenario where they are used. Again, they’re not tactical weapons- so anyone shooting them off is basically signing off on a nuclear exchange. That in turn means there’s no reason to hold back- they’d shoot all of them at once. And when it comes to nukes, even one getting through is enough to do you in. Thus, you’d have to stop all of them, which due to sheer quantity is practically impossible against any nuclear armed adversary except maybe North Korea.

There’s also no realistic way you’d stop ICBMs from being launched, in spite of what you might think from playing Call of Duty or whatever. They take so little time to prep these days that it’s just not practical. Most have a launch window measured in minutes.

Cruise missiles are overall easier to deal with, mainly due to the fact that they need something to bring them in range. As you can tell from the ‘Intercontinental’ part of the ICBM acronym, those can be launched from anywhere and hit anywhere else on the planet. Cruise missiles tend to be much shorter-ranged, especially the air-launched type. This means you can theoretically go after the launchers when you see them coming. In fact, you could be a bit proactive and hit those launchers before they even leave base, if you have the ability to do so.

Most cruise missiles fit into one of two categories: the subsonic terrain-hugging type, or the supersonic (or even hypersonic) high altitude type. While they can also be nuclear tipped, most current ones in use are not. Either way, they are usually not ballistic, which is good in some ways and bad in others. On the plus side, they don’t necessarily follow a predictable path. On the minus side, jamming can be effective against cruise missiles as many are GPS (or equivalent) guided. They often have fallback methods like inertial navigation, but this tends to be less accurate. Many also rely on terminal homing, against which decoys or other countermeasures may be effective.

Generally speaking though, the single biggest factor is that it’s a lot easier to get at cruise missiles at all points of deployment: both before launch, and during flight. They’re all air breathing, so you can use anything from interceptors to in some cases even old-school AAA guns to shoot them down. It is also a totally viable tactic to go after the launchers before they’ve fired, which would be ideal. The hardest part in dealing with them is detecting the incoming in time to do anything about it, which is just difficult but far from impossible.

Why were the British uniforms of WW2 so absolutely hideous, inefficient, uncomfortable and impractical?

The “Ugly” Uniform That Won Wars

There’s a common joke about how bad the British World War II uniform looked — boxy, scratchy, shapeless — and that’s true. It wasn’t fashionable. But the truth is, it wasn’t meant to be fashionable. The Battle Dress was protective equipment, not an outfit for your friends. You wore the Battle Dress to fight, not to be dapper.

That boxy shape? It’s pure survival logic. Loose enough to crawl . . . sprint . . . and dive without ripping. It had room for extra layers for a winter that wouldn’t let up. You didn’t look dapper in it — you looked alive. And in war, that’s the only look that mattered.

And it wasn’t comfortable. Wool itched like hell; but stayed warm when soaked, refused to melt in fire, and wouldn’t come off after weeks of mud and blood. The synthetic stuff would have burned right off your back.

The British designer was not trying to create the best looking uniform — they were solving problems. Were there operationally valid pockets on a paratrooper

smock? And of course, they began testing camouflage long before most armies thought of blending in. Everything was built to keep you in the fight a day longer.

“Ugly” is the point of view of a civilian; “function” is a soldier’s view. It wasn’t meant for photographs; it was meant for hell — perfect fight gear. You don’t win a war due to fashion.

How long is each propellant bag used in the main battery of an Iowa-class battleship?

Took a while to search this one out. During the investigation into the 1989 USS Iowa turret explosion, NSWC-Dahlgren (Naval Surface Warfare Center Dahlgren Division) measured 368 powder bags and determined that the average bag length was 16.536 inches, with a standard deviation of 0.178 inches. How’s that for precision?

That, of course is for a single propellant bag and the Iowa’s 16-inch/50-caliber Mark 7 gun typically used six (silk) bags per shot each, filled with 110 lb (50 kg) of propellant (I believe that five bag charges were used later). Therefore, the total length of the propellant for a six bag charge (excluding the projectile) would have been 99.21 inches uncompressed. The projectile length varied from around 64 inches to 72 inches, so the whole kit and caboodle would have been around 14 feet long.

Projectile and charge in the breech

The charge bags came in cans of three. The can is made out of aluminium for its light weight, corrosion resistance, and lack of sparking power. Each can held three 100 pound bags. On bottom of each bag was separate, quilted pouch with 0.79 lbs (0.36 kg) black powder bag igniter (primer patch). This served as an ignition point for the large, less reactive grains.

The D839 propellant grain used for full charges originally issued for this gun was 2 inches long (5.08 cm), 1 inch in diameter (2.54 cm) and had seven perforations (to help the grain burn evenly), each 0.060 inches (0.152 cm) in diameter with a web thickness range of 0.193 to 0.197 inches (0.490 to 0.500 cm) between the perforations and the grain diameter.

Powder bag coming through the flash-proof scuttle from the magazine and being loaded

The First and Second Powdermen for each gun would work the powder room scuttles and move the bags to the hoist. Which means that each man would have to squat down, pull the scuttle handle, stand up, move over, crouch down, pick up the bag – each weighing about 115 lbs. (52.2 kg) – and then walk the bag over to the hoist. Each of the two men would thus need to do this three times every 30 seconds in order to support a ROF of 2 rpmpg. During this same 30 seconds, each Third Powderman would need to operate the magazine scuttle and push a propellant bag over to the powder room scuttle six times. This sort of heavy work would quickly tire out all nine powdermen.
